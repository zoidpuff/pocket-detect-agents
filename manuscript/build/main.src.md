## Abstract

Finding ligand-binding pockets in protein structures is a basic step in structure-based biology, and fast tools such as P2Rank remain hard to beat. We report BURROW, a pocket finder that uses no neural network, and how it was made: AI agents planned, built and benchmarked it from a single prompt with minimal human input. BURROW places candidate points on a 1 Å grid, each standing for a small volume of space, and describes each point by ray-cast buriedness and by the chemistry of the first atom that each ray hits. Two gradient-boosted tree models score the points and rank the pockets. Claims and statistical tests were fixed before any test-set run, an independent agent audited the training data for leakage, and another recomputed the headline numbers. On LIGYSIS (2,775 chains, 6,721 non-redundant sites), BURROW's top-N+2 recall was 72.5% (version 2, 9.1 pockets per chain), against 60.4% for the best published method (fpocket+PRANK, 20.9 pockets per chain) and 52.6% for our re-run of P2Rank. Version 1, frozen before any test predictions existed, reached 64.3%. The difference between the versions comes from shorter output lists, which score better under the closest-pocket rule of the benchmark. Success within 4 Å of ligand atoms was about 54% in both versions, against 31.6%. Most of the margin came from ion sites; on non-ion sites the gain was +3.0 to +3.5 points (+3.1 at 4 Å). BURROW was non-inferior on HOLO4K and a temporal benchmark, and inconclusive on COACH420, apo/cryptic sites and dimer interfaces. An AlphaFold mode removed hallucinated pockets but did not beat a simple filter at pLDDT 70. Run as one process over 200 structures, BURROW took 142 ms per structure and P2Rank 209 ms; an optional fast preset took 48 ms. We list the goals that were not met.

## Author summary

Proteins bind small molecules in pockets on their surface, and finding these pockets is a first step in drug discovery and in understanding protein function. Many computer programs predict pockets, but fair comparisons between them are hard. Test proteins often resemble training proteins, benchmarks differ in what counts as a binding site, and the length of a program's output list can change its score. We asked whether AI agents, working from a single written instruction, could build a better pocket finder and test it honestly. A team of AI agents read the literature and wrote and reviewed a design. They also fixed their statistical tests in advance, built training data without overlap with the test proteins, and wrote and benchmarked the software. The resulting method, BURROW, finds more known binding sites than existing tools on a large benchmark and runs in about a tenth of a second per protein. The agents also showed where the advantage comes from: mostly metal-ion sites, and shorter output lists. They found no advantage on hidden (cryptic) pockets or on pockets between two protein chains. This single run shows what one agent-run project delivered and where human oversight would have helped.

## Introduction

This article reports both a method and an experiment. The experiment asked whether AI agents can run a computational biology project end to end, from the literature review to a benchmarked tool and a written report. All design, code, analyses and text in this study were produced by AI agents: one PI agent and the sub-agents that it launched. They worked from one prompt (S2 Text), with minimal human input (see the box above and Methods). We report the agents' method and results as a scientific contribution, and we also report how the agents worked, because the process affects how far the results can be trusted. In this article, "we" refers to the agent team.

The scientific task was to build a fast pocket finder that beats existing methods such as P2Rank, generalises across protein families and to apo and AlphaFold structures, handles homo- and heterodimers, and runs on both CPU and GPU (S2 Text). Ligand-binding pocket prediction has a long history. Geometric methods find concave regions from atom coordinates alone. Examples are LIGSITE, which counts protein–solvent–protein events along grid lines [@hendlich1997], PocketPicker, which casts rays to measure buriedness [@weisel2007], and fpocket, which clusters Voronoi alpha spheres [@leguilloux2009]. Knowledge-based methods learn which surface regions bind ligands. PRANK rescores fpocket pockets with a random forest on point features [@krivak2015], and P2Rank scores points on the solvent-accessible surface, clusters the positive points and ranks the clusters [@krivak2018]. Deep-learning methods such as DeepPocket, PUResNet, GrASP, VN-EGNN and IF-SitePred use 3D convolutions, graph networks or embeddings from protein language models [@aggarwal2022; @kandel2024; @smith2024; @sestak2025; @carbery2024].

Fair comparison of these methods is difficult. Utgés and Barton built LIGYSIS, a benchmark of 2,775 human protein chains whose binding sites are pooled across many PDB entries [@utges2024]. They re-ran 13 methods under one protocol and found that fpocket rescored by PRANK had the highest top-N+2 recall (60.4%; P2Rank 51.9%), and that no deep-learning method beat it. Their analysis also showed that fpocket's candidate list contains about 91% of the sites, whereas P2Rank's contains only 57% [@utges2024]. We read this as evidence that the room for improvement lies in two places: a candidate set with high recall, and a ranking of those candidates whose scores are comparable across proteins. Other benchmarks address other questions. COACH420 and HOLO4K are the legacy P2Rank test sets [@krivak2018; @yang2013; @schmidtke2010]. CryptoBench tests cryptic sites on apo structures [@skrhak2025], and PocketWatch uses a temporal split on predicted structures [@portnoi2026]. Leakage between training and test proteins is a recurring problem in this field [@skrhak2025; @portnoi2026; @durairaj2024].

AlphaFold models add new failure modes [@jumper2021; @varadi2022]. Low-confidence disordered regions can pack against the folded core and create pockets that do not exist. The authors of proteome-wide pocket atlases therefore remove pockets whose mean pLDDT is below 70 [@carpenter2025; @zillmer2025] (Zillmer and Walther also remove pockets with mean PAE above 10 Å), and AF2BIND removes terminal and internal low-confidence segments [@gazizov2026]. We found no public benchmark that tests pocket prediction on homo- and heterodimers.

Here we describe BURROW (buriedness-driven ultra-fast ranking of pockets, with confidence awareness). BURROW uses candidate points that each stand for a small volume of space, buriedness from rays cast through full-atom and backbone-only grids, and the chemistry of the first atom that each ray hits. It scores points and ranks pockets with gradient-boosted trees, and it has an AlphaFold mode that uses pLDDT. We pre-registered the claims and tests, reproduced the published LIGYSIS numbers before making any comparison, and had independent agents audit leakage and recompute the headline results. We report where BURROW is better, where it is not, and where its apparent gains come from properties of the benchmark rather than of the method.

## Results

### BURROW: high-recall candidates and a learned ranking

Fig 1 shows the pipeline. BURROW removes everything except protein atoms from the input and builds a 1 Å grid of points in the solvent shell around the protein. Each grid point stands for a small volume of space (a volume element), not a point on the protein surface. A cheap test based on LIGSITE [@hendlich1997] selects the buried points, which are kept at full resolution. Of the remaining points, only a coarser 3 Å sub-lattice is kept, so that shallow sites stay in the pool. Extra candidate points are placed where two or more protein atoms could coordinate a metal ion. Such sites are often missed by surface-point methods; metal-site location is also predicted by dedicated tools such as Metal3D [@durr2023].

@@FIG fig1_pipeline@@

Each point is described by 84 features. The main ones come from rays cast in fixed directions through two grids: one built from all heavy atoms and one built from backbone and Cβ atoms only. The fraction of blocked rays at several lengths measures buriedness on several scales. The backbone-only version is less sensitive to side-chain positions. For each ray, BURROW also records the chemical class of the first atom that the ray hits (apolar carbon, polar N or O, charged, or aromatic). We call this feature first-hit chemistry. It records which kinds of atoms a ligand at that point would contact, as a rough proxy for the cost of desolvation. Other features are local atom and residue counts, the weighted contact number as a measure of flexibility [@lin2008], protrusion [@krivak2018] and metal-coordination geometry.

A LightGBM classifier [@ke2017] scores the points. BURROW then forms candidate pockets on several scales, both from buried points and from high-scoring points. A second LightGBM model ranks the pockets. It is trained on cross-fitted point scores [@chernozhukov2018], so that the point scores in training follow the same distribution as at prediction time. Its training target is graded and decreases with the distance to the nearest true site. These two choices are meant to make scores comparable across proteins, so that pockets can be pooled and ranked across a proteome; pooled precision (P1K, below) tests this, but we did not analyse calibration directly. Suppression removes pockets whose centres are within 10 Å of a higher-ranked pocket. In the final version (v2), pockets with a ranker score below 0.03 are dropped once three have been kept (the output floor).

Two rankers share the same features. The `all` ranker was trained on ion and non-ion sites. The `small` ranker was trained on non-ion ligands only, which matches the ligand definitions of COACH420, HOLO4K, CryptoBench and PocketWatch. The mapping from benchmark to ranker was fixed before any results existed. Training data came from BioLiP2 [@zhang2024], with binding sites built in the same way as LIGYSIS sites and with every chain removed that is similar to any test chain (Methods). About 38% of the training sites contain only ions. Table 1 lists the published ideas that BURROW builds on.

### Benchmark design

Table 2 lists the test benchmarks. We pre-registered one primary claim and two follow-up claims on LIGYSIS (family 1, tested in a fixed sequence), and six secondary endpoints with a non-inferiority margin of −2 points and Holm correction (family 2) [@holm1979]. All claims were frozen in a pre-registration document before any test-set run. Three amendments were made later, each before the relevant predictions existed (Methods). Before trusting any comparison, the agents checked that our evaluation code reproduced all 33 published LIGYSIS method variants to within 0.4 points (gate: 0.5), and that our re-runs of P2Rank and fpocket+PRANK matched the published values to within 2 points. Confidence intervals (CIs) are 95% intervals from a paired bootstrap that resamples clusters of chains with at least 30% sequence identity.

@@TABLE benchmarks@@

Two versions of BURROW were scored on the test sets. Version 1 (v1) output the full suppressed list of up to 50 pockets. Version 2 (v2) added the output floor. v2 was chosen using development data before any v1 test score was computed, but 70 minutes after the files with the v1 predictions on the test sets had been written. We report both versions. The pre-registered claims hold for v1 alone.

### LIGYSIS: pre-registered claims pass for both versions

Family 1 passed in full for both versions, all three training seeds and both comparators (Fig 2, Fig 3A, Table 3). For v2, top-N+2 recall at a centre-to-centre distance (DCC) of at most 12 Å was 72.5%, 72.0% and 72.3% for the three seeds, against 60.4% for the published fpocket+PRANK predictions, on the 6,721 non-redundant sites that remain after the LIGYSIS authors' redundancy filter (6,882 sites in total). The smallest difference was +11.6 points, and the lowest CI bound was 9.4. v1 also passed, with 64.3–64.9% and a smallest difference of +3.9 [2.0, 6.0]. Our re-run of P2Rank 2.5.1 reached 52.6% (published value for P2Rank: 51.9%).

@@FIG fig2_ligysis@@

In the stricter test (C1-strict), a site counts as found only if the pocket closest to the ligand atoms lies within 4 Å of them (DCA ≤ 4 Å) and has a rank of N+2 or better. On this test, BURROW v2 reached 54.0–54.2% against 31.6% for fpocket+PRANK and 29.8% for P2Rank. Pooled precision of the top 1,000 pockets (P1K) was 96.1–96.9% against 81.7%. P1K was pre-registered as a non-inferiority test; its CI [10.2, 19.0] also excludes zero, but superiority was not a pre-registered test. The best published P1K on LIGYSIS, 94.3% for a rescored IF-SitePred variant, is 1.8 points lower than BURROW's. We did not test that difference. An independent agent recomputed all headline numbers with no project code, and used the LIGYSIS authors' own code for C1. All values matched to within 0.2 points.

### Secondary endpoints: non-inferior on legacy and temporal sets, inconclusive on apo and interface sites

Fig 3B summarises family 2. For v2, BURROW was non-inferior to the best comparator on HOLO4K (+1.6 [−1.2, 4.3]) and on PocketWatch (+0.8 [−1.4, 2.9]). The PocketWatch decision was narrow. The p-value for non-inferiority was 0.003 and the Holm threshold 0.005; with 1,000 resamples, p-values are resolved only to 0.001. PocketWatch models carry no pLDDT (B-factor 100 everywhere), so `--af-mode` had no effect there. v1 had been significantly worse than P2Rank on PocketWatch (−2.7 [−4.9, −0.5]), but not beyond the margin. On the AlphaFold set, BURROW was superior to P2Rank's AlphaFold configuration (+13.1 [11.0, 15.7]). COACH420 (+1.6 [−3.0, 5.8]), CryptoBench apo structures (−2.6 [−8.6, 3.0]) and dimer-interface sites (−3.7 [−8.7, 0.9]) were inconclusive. No endpoint was significantly worse than the comparator.

### Most of the LIGYSIS margin comes from ion sites and shorter output lists

Two properties explain much of the LIGYSIS margin (Fig 4). The first is the composition of the benchmark (Fig 4C). About 38% of LIGYSIS sites (2,580 of 6,721) are ion sites. For v2, the difference to our re-run of fpocket+PRANK was +29.5 points on ion sites, +4.7 [2.0, 7.7] on nucleotide and cofactor sites, and only +2.2 [0.1, 4.3] on other small molecules. P2Rank and fpocket+PRANK were not trained to find ion sites, which probably explains much of this gap. On LIGYSIS with ion sites removed (4,141 sites, `small` ranker; post hoc), v2 was ahead of fpocket+PRANK by +3.0 to +3.5 points and of P2Rank by +5.4 to +5.9 points. At 4 Å (DCA4) on the same sites, v2 was ahead by +3.1 [1.5, 4.8] points against fpocket+PRANK and +6.7 [5.2, 8.4] against P2Rank. v1 was behind fpocket+PRANK on non-ion sites at 12 Å (−4.6 [−6.3, −2.9]).

@@FIG fig4_composition_tradeoff@@

The second property is a rule of the LIGYSIS protocol (Fig 4A and 4B). Each site is credited only to the pocket whose centre is closest to the site centre. If a low-ranked pocket lies closer than the ranked hit, the site counts as a miss. Long output lists therefore lose credit. The agents found this on dev data: on cryptic sites in the apo development set (CryptoBench training folds), v1 found a pocket within 12 Å in the top N+2 more often than P2Rank (77.8% against 72.3%), yet scored lower under the closest-pocket rule. In 403 of its misses, the pocket closest to the site lay within 12 Å but was ranked beyond N+2; only 5 misses had no pocket within 12 Å. v1 output about 42 pockets per structure on that set, against 4.5 for P2Rank. The v2 output floor shortened the LIGYSIS list from 32.9 to 9.1 pockets per chain. C1 rose from 64.3% to 72.5%, while C1-strict (54.4% to 54.2%) and P1K (95.7% to 96.1%) were unchanged. Coverage fell. Maximum recall over the whole list dropped from 99.3% to 84.2%. Recall at any rank within N+2 dropped from 80.1% to 76.9%, and one-to-one recall from 76.0% to 71.3%. The C1 gain from v1 to v2 is therefore an effect of output length, not of better ranking. The baselines were run with their default list lengths, and they might also gain from a tuned floor. C1-strict is robust to the output floor, but not to ligand class: its overall margin of +22.6 points shrinks to +3.1 on non-ion sites. For exhaustive pocket discovery, `--min-score none` restores the v1 list exactly.

### Runtime

Fig 5 shows accuracy against runtime, measured in one session on the same 200 LIGYSIS chains for every tool. We ran each tool as one process over the 200 chains and divided the total time, including start-up and file input and output, by 200. BURROW's default preset took 142 ms per structure, P2Rank 2.5.1 (batch mode, one thread) took 209 ms and fpocket 4.2.3 took 605 ms. Default BURROW is therefore about 1.5 times faster than P2Rank per process, which misses the design target of at least 5 times. An optional fast preset took 48 ms (about 4.4 times faster than P2Rank). It was added after the test runs and was not pre-registered. On dev data it cost 0.8 points of top-N+2 recall and 1.7 points of DCA4 recall against the default. On one warm CPU core, including parsing, the fast preset took 31.5 ms (about 6.6 times less than P2Rank's 209 ms, which includes Java start-up) and the default 99.2 ms. Only the fast preset therefore met the design target of at most 70 ms per structure per core; the default took 96.0 ms (median, excluding parsing). Over 10,000 structures, three GPUs processed 411 structures per second with the default preset, against 171 per second for 24 CPU workers. The node was shared with other users' jobs, and repeated sessions differed by up to about 1.3-fold (P2Rank 270 against 209 ms), so these ratios carry an uncertainty of about ±30%. The Numba CPU backend and the Triton GPU backend give output identical to the reference implementation. The human AlphaFold proteome (20,550 proteins) ran in 129 s on three GPUs in AlphaFold mode (frozen v1 model, before the final speed work).

### AlphaFold mode removes hallucinated pockets but is equivalent to a pLDDT filter

In AlphaFold mode, BURROW removes terminal segments with pLDDT below 50 and internal low-confidence segments of 7 or more residues, following AF2BIND [@gazizov2026]. It then makes atoms with pLDDT below 80 partly transparent, linearly down to full transparency at pLDDT 40, so that low-confidence atoms block fewer rays and do not exclude candidate points. The standard ranker is used unchanged. With uniform pLDDT of 90 or more, the output is byte-identical to the standard mode.

On the AlphaFold test set (2,526 human AFDB models, 6,502 LIGYSIS sites), BURROW in AlphaFold mode reached 64.5% top-N+2 recall, against 51.4% for P2Rank's AlphaFold configuration (Fig 6A). This difference, too, came mostly from ion sites: +32.1 [28.3, 36.1] on ion sites and +1.9 [−0.1, 3.9] on non-ion sites (post hoc). Against BURROW on the unmodified model, AlphaFold mode added +1.0 [0.1, 2.0] points of recall and reduced hallucinated pockets among the top N+2 from 2.80% to 0.12%. A hallucinated pocket here means a pocket with mean pLDDT below 50 that matches no site. However, a simple filter was equally good. This filter drops pockets with mean pLDDT below 70, as in HOTPocket and in the work of Zillmer and Walther [@carpenter2025; @zillmer2025]. AlphaFold mode was ahead of it by only +0.4 [−0.5, 1.3] points, and the filter had higher precision (+2.4 points P1K). The same equivalence held on development data. The geometric mechanisms remove hallucinated pockets, which the filter also does, and they add no recall beyond the filter.

@@FIG fig6_af_dimers@@

Precision on AlphaFold models was a weakness. BURROW's P1K on the AlphaFold set was 58.9% against 73.9% for P2Rank. The CHEN11-only ablation model (251 P2Rank training structures, no ion labels) reached 78.2%, which suggests, but does not show, that the low precision comes from the ion-heavy `all` training. An optional ranker that uses pLDDT descriptors raised P1K to 73.9%, but it lowered PocketWatch top-N+2 by 3.4 points and P1K by 14.6 points, where no pLDDT is available (descriptive; this recommendation is informed by test data). Two goals of the prompt were not met. PAE matrices could not be used, because the AFDB file endpoint returned HTTP 403. Side-chain tolerance reduced recall on dev and is off by default. It makes side-chain atoms partly transparent; at an opacity factor λ = 0.25 on all side chains, recall fell by 10.9 points. The AlphaFold test set is also biased towards holo-like models: every protein has PDB structures, the median local Cα RMSD to the PDB representative is 0.38 Å, and only 95 benchmark sites (89 scored) have mean pLDDT below 70.

### Dimers: better on non-interface sites, not better on interface sites

BURROW takes any number of chains as input and computes candidates, features and ranks on the whole assembly. Because we found no public benchmark of pockets in dimers, the agents built one from 400 PDB assemblies of LIGYSIS proteins (274 homomeric and 126 heteromeric complexes of 2–8 chains). Symmetric copies of a site are merged. For every method, predicted pockets whose residue sets overlap by a Jaccard index of 0.5 or more are merged, by a rule that does not use the site labels. With full-assembly input, BURROW recalled 84.0% of interface sites in the top N+2, against 87.7% for P2Rank (−3.7 [−8.7, 0.9], inconclusive; Fig 6B). On hetero-interface sites the difference was −6.1 (66 sites). On single-chain sites within assemblies BURROW was far ahead (+19.0 [13.2, 25.9]), and over all sites it reached 83.9% against 72.9%. Only 9 of the 243 interface sites are ion sites, so the interface endpoint is in effect a test on non-ion sites, where BURROW's margins are small elsewhere too. When each chain was given alone (descriptive), BURROW placed a pocket near 69.6% of interface sites, against 55.4% for P2Rank (+14.2 [9.0, 20.1]). These values are counted per chain copy (634 rows), so they cannot be compared directly with the assembly values. Counted per site, with a site found if any isolated chain finds it, BURROW lost little relative to assembly input on the 12 Å criterion (−1.2) but 11.1 points on the 4 Å atom-distance criterion. A missing partner chain therefore mostly reduces the precision of localisation. A development cycle that targeted interface sites (v3) did not pass its pre-fixed gate (S1 Text). The interface stratum is small (243 sites, 158 bootstrap units), so CIs are about ±5 points.

### Apo and cryptic sites: not shown

On CryptoBench apo structures, the v2 difference to P2Rank varied with the seed from −2.6 to +1.1, with CIs of about ±6 points. BURROW had higher recall at any rank within N+2 (79.6% against 74.8%) and higher P1K (38.5% against 30.1%), but lower closest-pocket top-N. On 233 paired CryptoBench sites, BURROW lost 10.7 points from holo to apo structures, against 7.7 for P2Rank. BURROW is therefore, if anything, more sensitive to the apo state. At the residue level, BURROW matched PocketMiner [@meller2023] on 35 apo chains (ROC-AUC 0.792 against 0.789), but this sample is small. Four training changes aimed at apo structures (AlphaFold models as training inputs, random removal of side chains, apo-oriented ranker features and apo PDB chains) gave no gain on dev (S1 Text). Allosteric sites were not evaluated.

### Controls and ablations

Controls and ablations support the main results (S1 Fig and S2 Fig). A model trained on shuffled labels fell to 35.9% LIGYSIS top-N+2 (−28.4 points against v1). This is about the level of a single pocket placed at the protein centroid (37.0%) and well above evenly spaced surface pockets (18.3%). BURROW's own pockets with randomly shuffled ranks scored 14.6% on dev, which shows that the gain lies in the learned ranking. We also retrained BURROW without any training protein that has a Foldseek TM-score [@vankempen2024] of 0.5 or more to a test chain, which removed 41% of the 8,184 training inputs (representative chains and assemblies). Results changed by amounts within noise on 8 of 9 test rows (8 benchmarks, with two metrics for LIGYSIS); the exception was COACH420 (−3.2 [−6.0, −0.3]). In the strata of fold novelty, the gains on LIGYSIS and the AlphaFold set were largest for folds that are new relative to the training set (S1 Fig). No single feature group was essential. The largest single loss was 2.5 points on dev when metal features were removed. Ablations used one seed; the seed-to-seed standard deviation on dev is about 0.7 points. To test whether our training data alone explain the gain, P2Rank was retrained on a random 25% of our training set (larger runs exceeded the memory limit). This model was worse than stock P2Rank on LIGYSIS (−6.7 points) and 26.6 points below BURROW v2. On HOLO4K and dimer interfaces it reached BURROW's level. Our training data therefore do not reproduce BURROW's largest gains, although the control is limited by its reduced data volume.

### The agent-run process

The project ran for about 22 h (from 14:54 on day 1 to 12:35 on day 2), including a 4.5 h pause and the extension granted by the user, and produced more than 130 git commits. The PI agent launched sub-agents for 25 roles during the project and 5 more for the release repository and this manuscript (Table 4), and it logged 20 dated decisions (D0–D19). Independent agents caught several errors that the producing agents had missed. The training-set builder's own leakage check passed, but an independent audit found residual leakage, which led to the removal of 186 entries. An exactness test found that a GPU kernel received a threshold in single precision, which changed outputs only for atoms at pLDDT exactly 70. The external report reviewer found that a filter bug had made one descriptive table use all dimer sites instead of interface sites. Reviewer agents also flagged that the v2 floor gained mainly by shortening the output list. Other failures were operational: a broad process-kill command stopped another agent's test job, and timestamps that agents wrote from memory were 20–50 minutes off. An API usage limit paused all agents for 4.5 h. The pause cost only wall-clock time, because long jobs ran as background processes that wrote their state to disk.

## Discussion

BURROW meets some of the goals of the prompt and misses others. On LIGYSIS it passed all three pre-registered tests against fpocket+PRANK, the best published method: superiority in recall at 12 Å and at 4 Å, and non-inferiority in precision (the precision CI also excluded zero). Against other published methods the comparison is descriptive. It is non-inferior on HOLO4K and PocketWatch, superior on the AlphaFold set, and modestly faster than P2Rank with its default settings. It does not show better generalisation to apo or cryptic sites, it is not better at dimer interfaces, and allosteric sites were not tested. Its AlphaFold mode removes hallucinated pockets but adds nothing over a pLDDT filter, and PAE was not used. The default preset also missed both speed targets of the design: at most 70 ms per structure per core (median 96 ms) and at least 5 times faster than P2Rank (about 1.5 times per process). Only the fast preset, added after the test runs, met the first target.

The size of the LIGYSIS margin should be read with care. Ion sites make up 38% of LIGYSIS, and on them BURROW gains about 30 points, probably because the baselines were not trained to find ions. On non-ion sites the margin is +3.0 to +3.5 points against fpocket+PRANK. The second caveat is output length. The closest-pocket rule gives less credit to long lists, and the v2 floor raised C1 by 8.2 points while leaving C1-strict and P1K unchanged. Because the baselines were not given a tuned floor, the v2 margin on C1 partly measures the policy on list length. The localisation margin is robust to the output floor (DCA4 +22.6 points against fpocket+PRANK, unchanged from v1), but it is not robust to ligand class. On LIGYSIS without ion sites (post hoc, `small` ranker) the DCA4 margin is +3.1 [1.5, 4.8] against fpocket+PRANK and +6.7 [5.2, 8.4] against P2Rank. The clearest gain on non-ion sites is therefore over P2Rank, not over fpocket+PRANK. Under the LIGYSIS protocol, interface sites are also judged on a single chain, and the 12 Å criterion is lenient. On dev, the median distance between a successful BURROW pocket and the site centre is 3.3 Å, against 4.7 Å for P2Rank.

Where does the gain come from? On LIGYSIS, the candidate set has 99.3% maximum recall before the floor (at 32.9 pockets per chain), against 57.7% for P2Rank (about 5 pockets per chain; fpocket reaches 91% at 20.9), but candidates alone are not enough: geometry-only ranking is at P2Rank's level. With the same ranker, BURROW's own candidates gave 16.5 points more DCA4 recall on dev than fpocket's candidates. Both parts are needed, as the literature analysis suggested [@utges2024]: candidates with high recall and precise centres, and a learned ranking whose scores can be pooled across proteins. Fold-disjoint retraining is evidence against memorisation of folds. However, the training labels were built in the same way as LIGYSIS sites, which may give BURROW an advantage on LIGYSIS over benchmarks with other site definitions.

Several decisions shaped the results (Table 5). The weakest point is the timing of v2. The floor was derived on training and dev data and adopted before any v1 test score was computed. However, v1 prediction files had existed for 70 minutes, and the independent verifier could not rule out that they had been inspected. The agents' session transcripts are not part of the repository, so this cannot be checked from the archived record. The pre-registered claims hold for v1 alone, which limits the damage. Ideally, however, the policy on list length would have been fixed before the first test run. The agents disclosed this and other test-informed steps: the interface cycle v3 was motivated by a test result (and changed nothing), and the advice to use the optional AlphaFold ranker relies on descriptive test rows. The agents did not switch defaults on test evidence. For example, the fast preset was better than the default on CryptoBench test data (+6.7 points), but the default was kept.

@@TABLE decisions@@

The experiment also shows what agent-run research can and cannot do yet. The agents followed rules that a careful human group would follow: reproduce published numbers first, pre-register, audit data independently, use controls, and report negative results (S1 Text). Applying them caught real errors. These weaknesses also occur in human projects that run under time pressure. Claims were fixed before the agents had broken down the benchmark by ligand class. The policy on list length was decided after the first test predictions existed. Some branches were explored past the point of diminishing returns. The agents' self-reports needed independent checks. Human oversight would be most useful at three points: when the claims are fixed, when a test result is first seen, and when deciding how strongly to state a result. This was a single run of one agent system on one task. We did not repeat it or compare it with a human team, so we cannot say how often such a run succeeds or how its quality compares.

Several follow-ups are open: a tuned, pre-registered output floor for the baselines, which is the most direct test of the output-length caveat; a context-dependent output floor (on dev, 65% of v2's non-ion misses were pockets that the global floor removed), a larger interface benchmark with more multimer training data, cheap flexibility models for apo sites, PAE through bulk download, and timing on an exclusive node.

## Materials and methods

### Agent-based project execution

**Setup.** The project ran on one shared compute node (64-core AMD EPYC 9554, four RTX 4090 GPUs, an effective memory cap of about 79 GB) inside a Singularity container on the ETH Euler cluster. Local copies of the PDB [@berman2000] and the AlphaFold database [@varadi2022] were mounted read-only. The agents used Claude Code with Claude Opus 5.5 and Claude Sonnet 5.5 on 26–27 October 2026. The Claude Code version, tool settings and token usage were not recorded. Two writing and plotting guides ("skills") were used in the manuscript phase: edit-for-clarity for the text and dataviz for the figures. The human's prompt (S2 Text) set the goals, a 24 h budget, the hardware, and process rules: design through repeated independent critique, review of every result by an agent that did not produce it, a research tree as memory, a lessons file, and end-to-end reproducibility.

**Roles.** One PI agent (Opus, one long session) planned, delegated, reviewed and integrated. It owned the design, the pre-registration amendments, the research tree with its decision log, the lessons file and all git commits. It did not write method code. Each sub-agent was a fresh Claude Code agent with its own context, launched with a written brief that stated the goal, the files it owned, the deliverables and the rules. Sub-agents could be resumed with follow-up messages, and several roles were served by the same long-lived agent that was resumed many times (for example the evaluation engineer, the method developer and the geometry and HPC engineer). Table 4 lists 30 roles: 25 during the project and 5 for the release repository and this manuscript. The literature reviewer also used two research sub-agents. Opus was used for most roles, and Sonnet for the baselines engineer, the leakage auditor and the process retrospectives. Agent briefs and session transcripts were not archived, so individual delegation steps cannot be audited. The human sent four messages after the prompt (S2 Text); the environment restarted the session twice with an empty "Continue" message.


**Design by critique.** A literature agent wrote a review of methods, benchmarks and metrics. The PI drafted a design, which six independent reviewer agents assessed (an external PI, prior work and data, statistics, algorithms and compute, failure modes of AI agents, and biophysics and AlphaFold). A second round used two reviewers. Each reviewer proposed concrete changes or stated that none were needed. The design has a table that maps each review to the changes it caused. Reviews were capped at two rounds, although the prompt allowed three.

**Independence and gating.** Four checks were done by agents that had not produced the checked work: the leakage audit (re-implemented from scratch), the review of dev results, the recomputation of headline test numbers (without project code, and with the LIGYSIS authors' code for C1), and the review of the report. The method, the dimer evaluator and the benchmarks themselves were not re-implemented. A script checks each of the 1,724 tagged numbers in the project report against the committed result files. Test sets could be scored only through a gated script that refuses modified files, records the model checksum and appends to a test ledger. At most three frozen versions could be scored. Predictions for frozen versions were generated from isolated git worktrees, so that ongoing development could not change them. Each agent owned a list of files, and only the PI committed.

**Interruptions.** The session restarted twice in the first two hours, and an API usage limit paused all agents for about 4.5 h overnight. Jobs longer than 5 minutes ran detached with logs and completion markers, and agents were resumed from their files, so no work was lost. Two fresh agents ran process retrospectives ("what should we stop doing?" and "what worked well?"), and the PI condensed the lessons into 15 rules.

**Manuscript.** This manuscript was written by a sub-agent from the verified report and result files. Other sub-agents made the figures (each plotted value read from a committed result file), checked the references against Crossref and PubMed, and reviewed the draft as a referee; the draft was then revised. A separate sub-agent built the release repository. A writing guide based on Clark, Martin and Mahowald [@clark2026] was applied to every sentence. Every number in the text was checked by script against the project report and result files.

### Input and candidate points

BURROW reads PDB or mmCIF files with gemmi [@wojdyr2022] and keeps protein heavy atoms (first model, first alternate location). Ligands, waters, ions, glycans and nucleic acids are removed, so predictions do not depend on HETATM records (tested). Geometry is computed in a canonical frame given by the principal axes. Grid points (1 Å spacing) are kept if they are at least the van der Waals radius plus 1 Å from every atom and within 6 Å of the nearest atom. For each point, BURROW counts LIGSITE protein–solvent–protein events along 7 directions up to 8 Å [@hendlich1997]. Points with a count of 2 or more are kept at full resolution (volume 1 Å³), and the rest on a 3 Å sub-lattice. Metal candidates are placed at 1.95–2.35 Å from a side-chain atom that can coordinate a metal. Coordinating atoms are His ND1/NE2, Cys SG, Asp or Glu carboxylate O, Met SD and backbone O. A candidate is kept if at least two coordinating atoms (at least one from a side chain) lie within 2.6 Å of it and no other atom lies closer than 2.4 Å. They are deduplicated at 1.5 Å, with at most one per cluster of coordinating atoms.

### Point features

Rays in 30 fixed Fibonacci directions (60 in the accurate preset) are marched through two absorption grids: all heavy atoms, and backbone plus Cβ atoms only. The occlusion radius is the van der Waals radius plus 0.5 Å. For each grid, BURROW computes the blocked fraction at ray lengths of 6, 10 and 16 Å (plus 24 Å in the accurate preset), the mean depth of the first hit, the fractions of first hits by chemical class, and the escape fraction. Other features are the protein–solvent–protein count, the nearest-atom distance, a metal flag with the number and angular spread of coordinators within 2.8 Å, atom counts at 4–12 Å, counts of 13 atom classes and 7 residue groups at 4 Å and their fractions at 8 and 12 Å, the mean weighted contact number (sum of 1/d² over Cα atoms) at 8 and 12 Å, and protrusion (atoms within 10 Å). This gives 84 features (86 in the accurate preset). The geometric value of a point, b, is the mean blocked fraction at 10 Å, 16 Å and the longest ray length (so 16 Å counts twice in the default preset; 10, 16 and 24 Å in the accurate preset).

### Point scorer and training labels

The point scorer is a LightGBM binary classifier [@ke2017] with 250 trees of 63 leaves, bagging and feature fractions of 0.8, and fixed seeds. For the `all` ranker, a point is positive if it lies within 4 Å of a non-ion ligand atom or within 3 Å of an ion. For the `small` ranker, it is positive if it lies within 4 Å of a non-ion ligand atom. Training samples 30% of positive and 3% of negative points per structure. In the default preset, only points with b ≥ 0.7 and metal candidates are scored (about 45% of points). Gradient-boosted trees were chosen because they are fast on tabular features and because the prompt warned that large deep-learning models overfit. Most hyperparameters (grid spacing, number and length of rays, thresholds, tree sizes) were set in early runs on the P2Rank training sets (CHEN11 and JOINED) and then chosen among about ten settings on dev under the speed budget of decision D5; ranker variants were selected on training-set cross-validation. Dev numbers are therefore somewhat optimistic; test sets were not used for any choice.

### Pocket formation, ranking and suppression

Candidate pockets are the union of a geometric pass (single-linkage components at 2.05 Å of points with b ≥ 0.40, 0.55, 0.70 or 0.85) and a score pass (point score ≥ 0.15, 0.30 or 0.50). Components longer than 20 Å are split by weighted k-means into pieces of about 12 Å, and components longer than 10 Å are also split into pieces of about 6 Å. Pockets smaller than 10 Å³ are dropped (metal candidates excepted). The centre is the centroid of the points weighted by volume and geometric value. The pocket ranker is a LightGBM model (300 trees, 15 leaves) on 76 descriptors: aggregates and quantiles of point scores, volume, buriedness (including backbone-only), first-hit chemistry, residue groups, shape from principal components, number of residues and chains, and protein size. Its training uses cross-fitted point scores from 5 folds of 30% sequence clusters. The ranker is trained with cross-entropy on the target clip(1 − q/12, 0, 1), where q is the smallest DCC to a site whose ligand atoms lie within 4 Å of the pocket centre. Suppression is greedy in order of ranker score. A pocket is removed if its centre is within 10 Å of a kept pocket, its residue set has a Jaccard index of 0.75 or more with one, or its point overlap with one is 0.3 or more. At most 50 pockets are kept. In v2, pockets with score below τ = 0.03 are dropped once three have been kept. τ was chosen by cross-validation over clusters of the training set, under the condition that closest-pocket recall, DCA4 recall, one-to-one P1K and top-N must not decrease. Reported residues are those with a heavy atom within 4 Å of a pocket point.

### Presets, AlphaFold mode and multimers

The default preset (30 rays, 3 Å sub-lattice, partial scoring) was used for all pre-registered claims. The accurate preset (60 rays, 2 Å sub-lattice, all points scored) is shipped because it was more than 1 point better on non-ion dev sites. The fast preset (1.5 Å grid, 12 rays, 2 Å sub-lattice, a point model of 100 trees with 31 leaves) was added after the test runs. It was selected on training-set cross-validation with a rule fixed in advance (at most 35 ms per structure and at most a 2-point loss) and confirmed once on dev.

`--af-mode` takes residue pLDDT from the B-factor column of the Cα atom. It removes terminal runs with pLDDT < 50 and internal runs of 7 or more residues with pLDDT < 50 [@gazizov2026]. Remaining atoms get an opacity w = clip((pLDDT − 40)/40, 0, 1) in both absorption grids, and atoms with w < 0.5 do not exclude candidate points. The standard models are used unchanged. An optional ranker (`--af-ranker`) adds pocket pLDDT descriptors and was trained on AFDB models of training proteins with transferred labels. A structure-only stand-in for inter-domain uncertainty, based on spectral bisection of the Cα contact graph, gave no gain and is not used.

Multimers need no special mode. All chains enter the grids, features and ranker, and one pocket descriptor is the number of chains that line the pocket.

### CPU and GPU backends

The reference implementation (NumPy [@harris2020], SciPy [@virtanen2020]) is the specification. The fast backend uses Numba on the CPU [@lam2015] and Triton/PyTorch kernels on the GPU [@tillet2019; @paszke2019], with ray marching in double precision and a tree-inference routine that does not depend on LightGBM. GPU runs use one process per GPU, with CPU workers for parsing and post-processing. Outputs (scores, centres, residues and points) were identical to the reference on 64 random dev structures for four models on CPU and GPU, and on 40 AlphaFold models in AlphaFold mode. A bug that passed one threshold to a GPU kernel in single precision was found after the v1 freeze. It changed outputs only for atoms at pLDDT exactly 70. It was fixed with a regression test, and all v1 AlphaFold-mode test predictions were generated on the CPU path.

### Training and development data

Training data came from BioLiP2 (snapshot 2025-11-12) [@zhang2024]: X-ray or EM structures at 3.0 Å or better, receptor chains of at least 50 residues, and BioLiP2's relevant ligands including metal ions. Before the training set was built, the agents froze and checksummed the union of all test sets. This union covered LIGYSIS, all polymer entities of COACH420 and HOLO4K, all CryptoBench splits, PocketMiner, the PocketWatch test set, the AlphaFold set and every dimer chain. A training chain was removed if an MMseqs2 search [@steinegger2017] found any hit to the test union with E < 10⁻³ and identity of at least 30% over at least 50% coverage or at least 80 aligned residues, or if it shared a PDB ID or UniProt accession with a test entry. This removed 77.4% of BioLiP2 chains. Sites were built in the LIGYSIS style [@utges2024]. For each UniProt accession, a representative chain was chosen, and ligands from up to 20 other entries were superposed onto it by local, sequence-anchored superposition (Cα RMSD ≤ 2 Å). Ligands were then clustered by the relative intersection of their binding-residue fingerprints with average linkage, cut at 0.5. Clashing non-ion ligand atoms were removed. Before the audit fix, the training set had 14,073 sites, of which 37.7% contained only ions. Dev data were 10% of sequence clusters, built in the same way and also leakage-filtered against training. The independent audit found 7 assembly-partner chains that violated the rule, 92 evaluated inputs that violated it when sequences of observed residues were used (88 of them at 30–35% identity), and one UniProt overlap. Of these, 8 were hard failures; the agents removed every entry that any tier of the audit flagged (186 entries, 2.6%), which left 6,475 training and 579 dev chains. No full re-audit was run after this fix. The test verifier later found none of the 2,565 LIGYSIS UniProt accessions in training or dev data.

### Benchmarks, metrics and baselines

Table 2 lists the benchmarks. LIGYSIS was used with the authors' ligand-free representative chains and their redundancy filter, which leaves 6,721 of 6,882 sites for scoring [@utges2024]. Under top-N+2 recall, a site counts as found if the pocket whose centre is closest to the site centre has DCC ≤ 12 Å and rank ≤ N+2, where N is the number of sites on the chain. C1-strict uses ligand atoms placed on the representative chain by superposition (6,387 of 6,721 sites). P1K is the precision of the 1,000 highest-scoring pockets pooled over the dataset. COACH420 and HOLO4K were scored with P2Rank's rules (DCA ≤ 4 Å, top-n) using the explicit relevant-ligand (mlig) versions [@krivak2018]; our evaluator reproduced P2Rank's own evaluator to 0.1%. The AlphaFold set maps LIGYSIS sites onto AFDB v6 models by UniProt numbering, after a check of residue types. The dimer set uses assembly 1 of entries of LIGYSIS proteins, restricted to assemblies defined by both authors and software, X-ray or EM at 3 Å or better, and 2–8 protein chains. A site is at an interface if 3 or more residues on each of 2 or more chains contact it. Symmetric copies are grouped at a residue-set Jaccard index of 0.5 or more, and every method's predictions are collapsed by the same rule before scoring. scPDB [@desaphy2015] was not used as a test set because it has no stated license, one site per entry, and overlap with LIGYSIS. AF2BIND data were not used because they are residue-level with about 65 test chains, and HOTPocket because it is a catalogue of predictions, not a benchmark.

Comparators were run on identical, cleaned inputs: P2Rank 2.5.1 (default, and its AlphaFold configuration on predicted structures) [@krivak2018] and fpocket 4.2.3 rescored by PRANK [@leguilloux2009; @krivak2015]. On LIGYSIS we used PRANK 2.4.2, as in the original paper. Both versions gave identical LIGYSIS numbers. Published per-chain predictions of the deep-learning methods on LIGYSIS were scored with our harness and are descriptive, because they have no paired CI. Several deep-learning tools were also wrapped and run on some sets, but their training data overlap with the legacy benchmarks, so they were descriptive only (S1 Text).

### Statistics

The bootstrap resampled sequence clusters (30% identity, MMseqs2) 1,000 times with a fixed seed. Multi-chain structures mapped to the union of their chains' clusters. All differences are paired, failures count as misses, and P1K is recomputed in each resample. Family 1 had three requirements, each for every seed and against both the published and our re-run fpocket+PRANK: for C1, a difference of at least +2 points with CI > 0; for C1-strict, CI > 0; and for C2, a lower CI bound above −3. Family 2 used a non-inferiority margin of −2 points against the better of P2Rank 2.5.1 and fpocket+PRANK, with Holm correction over six endpoints [@holm1979]. Strata (fold novelty by maximum Foldseek TM-score to training, ligand class, pLDDT band and dimer site class) were pre-registered as descriptive. Analyses labelled post hoc were added after the test runs. The pre-registered sensitivity analysis on the common chain subset was run (C1, v2 seed 0: +11.8 [9.7, 13.8] on 6,700 sites). The pre-registered sensitivity analysis with UniProt accessions as bootstrap units was not run.

### Pre-registration, amendments and test versions

The pre-registration was frozen at 15:39 CET on day 1 (git time), before any test-set run. Amendment A1 changed the grouping of symmetric dimer copies to a Jaccard index of 0.5 or more, before any method output existed. Amendment A2 defined how ligand atoms are placed for C1-strict. Amendment A3 changed the fpocket version of the reproduction gate to 4.2.3, which matches the LIGYSIS authors' pocket counts. A2 and A3 were made before any BURROW test prediction. Test v1 was the frozen model at feature freeze. Test v2 added the output floor (decision D13). The planned third version (v3, interface sites) and a later per-region floor (v3b) both failed their pre-fixed dev gates, so v2 is final. Twelve models (3 seeds × 2 rankers × 2 presets) were frozen per version.
